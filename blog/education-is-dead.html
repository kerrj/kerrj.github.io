Education is dead, long live education!

Thoughts on how AI wrecks education, and how we might adapt.

I was walking on Berkeley's campus one afternoon behind two undergraduates talking to each other. "How's your pset going?" one said. "Oh, not bad. I kinda drafted some answers and then I asked chat for the rest."1 This was maybe the very beginning of 2024, and at the time I just sort of scoffed, almost nostalgically remembering how time-crunched undergrad felt. It was just a weird reminder that the stuff we read and write papers about actually is starting to impact everyone around me. Later, I quickly realized the real gravity of this comment when I TA'd undergrad intro to CV. I had always been somewhat interested in how LLMs affect students (I couldn't imagine having such a Pandora's box in my pocket during my college years), but now, after a semester, I think this is going to be way more disruptive than I originally realized, and we're woefully unprepared for it in higher education.

1 Chat, for the unaware reader, is what the young-uns call ChatGPT, or more broadly just your favorite neighborhood LLM.

Is this just another anti-AI rant? No! The title of this blog is of course a callback to what Europeans used to shout when a new king rose, recognizing the passing of an old king and the ascension of a new one. Similarly, I think some parts of education will have to die, and others require some rethinking to get right, but with some growing pains we can crown a new king. I'm actually also pretty positive about AI in general (maybe that's just cope as someone working on it), and I think long term I'm pretty optimistic about the outlook of education. This past semester I fully internalized just how big a deal AI is, and I think right now people aren't dedicating enough time to thinking about how to adapt higher education to this new... twist. I'll also mostly write about higher education here, but I'd imagine there's a good amount of overlap with more important grades2 like high school.

2 Subject worthy of a whole other post.

The promise of AI

First we need to talk about why so many people are hyped about AI for education. To give you an idea how pervasive this is, when I was at Google DeepMind I was lucky to be at an intern round-table with Jeff Dean, and someone asked him what he thinks the most exciting applications of AI were. I think one was medicine, and another was education. Sure, this is "just" one guy, but I think this optimism towards education technology ("ed-tech") is generally blooming among the people making LLMs. Many LLMs have a "study" mode or something like that, which is supposed to help you learn material rather than just giving an answer.

In my head there are 2 main types of pitches of increasing ambition: the first regarding helping in-person teachers right now, and the second towards fundamentally reshaping education.

  1. We all know that teachers are underpaid, underfunded, and overworked, right? So wouldn't it be great if LLMs could come in and save them? Imagine a world where every student gets instant, personalized help! Every teacher can develop their course materials at a fraction of the effort! Grading is fast, feedback is more consistent, and assignments can even be interactive back-and-forths! No more queues in office hours, no more day-long Piazza response times, and no more pesky logistics for teachers to waste time on.
  2. Some might go even further and fantasize about education beyond the classroom: now everyone in the world can access the same quality of education anywhere! We could democratize education to every corner of the earth, helping lift up everyone and distributing knowledge, wealth, power; a higher standard of living for all of humanity.

Sounds pretty great, right? To be honest, I think a lot of it isn't even that far-fetched. I feel like within a decade or so it's not unreasonable to incorporate a lot of these improvements somehow. BUT, nobody really has a good proposal for how to get there, and unfortunately the genie is somewhat already out of the bottle. The unfortunate reality of the situation is that AI right now is disrupting the way students learn, the way teachers evaluate them, and the way teachers must structure their classes to ensure fairness. And (understandably) nobody really knows what to do about it. I'm throwing my hat in the ring here with some jumbled thoughts.

What's the point of higher education?

It doesn't make sense to talk about any of this without thinking about the point of all this in the first place. Then we can think about how to rebuild around these shared goals. Higher education has 5 main goals to me, in order of priority:

  1. Teaching students how to work with, share ideas with, and argue with other people.

    This is head and shoulders the most important thing, and it's pretty commonly echoed, so I won't labor it. We live in a society (lol), and therefore the most important life skills are those that allow you to function in a community of peers and get what you want out of other people. I put this bluntly, but that's not to say we're creating psychopaths, but nurturing open-minded, convincing, respectful people who are empowered to go out and do what they want.

  2. Teaching students how to learn, that is, helping them develop an understanding of how to go from 0 to 80% on any subject they want.

    Most of the knowledge you learn in undergrad is probably not going to be useful in as little as 10 years, but the real point is that you've gone through how to learn about a field once, and now you can do it again when you need to. In addition, you should have a shallow bit of knowledge in a lot of stuff, which helps make connections across fields (and work with people better).

  3. Teaching students how to think.

    Whether it's a STEM field or not, college should teach problem solving and engaging with subject matter at a deeper level than knowledge. For example, considering and proposing hypotheticals, understanding some background and motivation for why and how people acquired the knowledge, and questioning material with a healthy cynicism and skepticism.

  4. Teaching students how to be good to each other.

    An important part of education I think is often glossed over, which is the sheer humanity of it. Teaching integrity, kindness, resilience, generosity, and other traits you'd want to instill in a generation that's about to be released into the real world and become colleagues and leaders.

  5. Teaching students a little taste of broad subjects, and deeper expertise in one field.

    Finally, the reason most students think they're here (tragically). The last part of higher education is to make sure that you're ready to go into a career, which varies massively based on what field you specialize in, but generally involves pouring knowledge into people's brains which will enable them to join something important and do good work, whether that's research, industry, helping people, or continuing training. After undergrad, a student should be able to eat lunch with a top researcher in their field and ask intelligent questions, and have enough grounding in fundamentals to pretty quickly fill in the gaps of their knowledge with help from the researcher.

There's one last implicit point of higher education:

  1. Provide some reliable verification to the outside world that a student has achieved the above goals successfully.

    We'll come back to this point later, but without it the whole education system crumbles, since we can no longer trust that students coming out of an institution are ready for the world.

To sum up:

Most goals of education have nothing to do with the material itself.

Unfortunately, I think too many teachers have this priority list completely reversed, and focus on dumping information into people's brains rather than cultivating people who can use their brains well. If you've ever had an amazing teacher, which I hope everyone gets to experience at least once, then think back and try to remember what about them was different, and now, maybe years later, what you actually remember about them. Chances are it has nothing to do with the content itself, but what really "sticks" with you is something meta: a way of approaching problems, a classroom that inspired you to interact with other people. I want to share three of my best teachers here to illustrate that point.

One was an 8th grade English teacher named Mr. Hansman who, twice a semester, would arrange a class-wide debate, working in groups of threes to research an argument and stand up and run a full debate. Students got into it, and somehow even over a decade later I remember how much it helped me grow in the first 4 bullet points. Unfortunately he retired and last I heard runs a pottery studio (teaching is draining; it's a shame the best teachers are often the ones that burn out the fastest).

The second was Steve Shapiro, a physics prof I had during high school (I went to a nerd school with dual-enrollment college classes). Steve was one of those blessed teachers with infinite patience, and would stand there and keep accepting "why" questions until you got down to some unknowable axiom of the universe. I've forgotten most of what I used to know about complicated stuff, but the class once again prioritized teaching from the perspective of building up knowledge from basic principles, and understanding the experiments that supported that understanding.

The third was my theoretical CS prof at CMU, Anil Ada. Anil was universally loved, which is commendable given he teaches one of the hardest required undergrad classes. Again, the most memorable part about his class was the structure of homeworks, part of which forced you to work in groups of people to solve a set of proofs each week. The psets also emphasized using proof tools and algorithmic techniques over memorizing things, and really drilling down deeply on topics in lecture to give you a small set of tools to practice thinking about in homeworks. Again, de-emphasizing (5) over the first three.

Teachers are hypocrites, necessarily

When I was in middle school, I remember one of my teachers spending an entire class talking about the ultimate boogeyman: Wikipedia. Of course it seems ridiculous now, but back then it held a certain disdain from teachers on their tongues, scorning it like a tainted source that would destroy our brains if we used it instead of doing the real ethical research and finding primary sources. So why aren't LLMs just the latest evolution in this game? Maybe educators telling students to not go to it for help is similar to my grade school teachers forcing us to not use Wikipedia. After all, we all use it in our daily lives, so why high-road students and shame them for trying to use it for homework?

Of course, the answer is that sometimes to get the point across, you have to knee-cap students a bit to force them to go through the right exercise. We do this all the time: teaching kids times tables instead of letting them use a calculator, or forcing high schoolers to learn integrals rather than just plug them into a symbolic engine. In LLMs' case, there's no difference in forcing students to be a bit archaic (gasp, reading manually) to better learn how to think, communicate, or engage with material or other students. It can be a difficult tradeoff though, as students understandably will feel scared about falling behind the others that may lean heavily on LLMs and as a result get more opportunities, higher grades, take more classes, etc. I don't have a good way to solve this problem though, since unfortunately it might require tools that don't exist to restrict usage to an acceptable level.

Higher education is dead

The crisis we face in higher education right now is that AI has immense risk to obliterate the first four goals. We've been through shifts in technology time and time again with calculators, personal computers, the internet, hell, even pen and paper over quills and etc., so what's different this time? The difference all comes down to the fact that most prior technologies could only automate information lookup or niche mechanical computations, but LLMs can automate cognition itself. The internet couldn't solve your proof (unless an exact copy existed), it couldn't write your essay, it couldn't design your code. There's also something that feels a little less wrong about using chatbots for help, a bit less copy-pasty and more like asking a friend for help, that makes LLMs more of a slippery slope. If you don't copy the solution, just use the idea, what's the problem, right?

Unfortunately, learning to think requires struggle, and if you automate away the struggle of thinking, then you can't really learn how to grow that mental muscle. This is why giving help in office hours is so difficult: you have to somehow nudge students along towards the right solution without spoiling it, or you destroy the whole point of the homework. If a student just uses an LLM like a 24/7 TA, they're not cheating per se, but they've undone the struggle of homework and made it so they no longer have to think to get a good grade. They'll never learn how to autonomously generate ideas, evaluate them, and persist at this until they hone in on an answer.

Constant access to an on-demand TA is just as dangerous for learning as cheating itself.

In fact, there's a significant benefit to the scarcity of help: it forces you to talk to your fellow students to learn together, and it forces you to chew on content on your own, which helps you think better. Maybe this is a controversial take, but I think the students who spend every hour in office hours doing homework probably developed a shallower understanding of the assignments than the ones who sparingly attended when they were truly stumped. This same effect is on steroids now that everyone has a TA in their pocket.

Wolfram Alpha made basic calculus like taking integrals obsolete, but if you treat LLMs like Wolfram Alpha, then it would make thinking itself obsolete.3 This is disastrous for students, not just because it means their understanding will probably be shakier since they got too much information fed to them, but they failed to get many of the main takeaways from college: thinking, reasoning, and self-studying. Even worse, they did it without practicing the most important thing in college: interacting with people.

3 Amazingly, there's probably a non-trivial number of AI zealots that are OK with this! "In 10 years, we won't need people to think anymore anyway, so what's the point of teaching people how to do it?" I'm not going to humor this position here though; I think not teaching young people how to think is self-evidently a horrible idea.

The pocket-TA problem

To understand how harmful a pocket TA can be, consider a fictional student for a sec, named Chad GPT. Chad goes through college immediately asking a chatbot for approaches on how to do homework, uses it to explain lecture content to him, asks for practice questions to study for the exam, and uses it for all the coding and writing assignments. He doesn't cheat, i.e., he doesn't copy-paste the solutions right off; he makes sure he sort of understands it before paraphrasing and typing up. For code he gets the important bits, the overall structure, and debugging help from chat, but does fill in most of the code himself. Chad never works with other people. Why would he? He quickly finishes homework, usually gets near-perfect scores, then turns to all the other fun things to do in college. Chad is going to be our north star for the moment.

Should Chad GPT graduate college?

I think he shouldn't. If you disagree, then I would argue you don't value goals 1–4 of higher education nearly as much as you should. Sure, maybe Chad would learn course content at an OK level, enough to pass exams and maybe even enough to retain knowledge for a career afterwards. But he missed out on the vast majority of the main goals of higher education (to remind you: working with people, independent thinking, being good to others, learning to learn). In a world where all students are Chad, there is really no point of them being at a university. The benefits of being at a university are first and foremost being part of a community of learners, and learning how to interface in such a society. The risk of AI pocket TAs is that they silo students into isolation, which stifles their ability to interact in a society. In addition, if everyone is learning from the same model weights, it's likely we'll see a real mode collapse in thinking among new students, who never had to interact with meaningfully different entities and all got the same coaching from the same model.

Would Chad GPT graduate college?

The uncomfortable thing is, for lots and lots of classes, Chad would completely ace the homeworks. The only thing holding Chad back from getting an A+ in every class is written exams, but maybe even those he could pass by cramming sufficiently with chat-generated practice problems and templates to remember for solving most of the types of questions an exam could ask. After the exams, he probably quickly forgets information he crammed from before.

This thought experiment should raise some eyebrows about evaluation in college. If a student passes a class, we trust that they at least somewhat achieved the goals set out. And yet, clearly we have a case where a non-functioning citizen (Chad) can pass lots of classes with flying colors without really learning the core goals, despite not technically "cheating." Over many years we've set up evaluations with some reasonable prior on how humans behave, such that for someone to pass college, they probably would have had to learn how to think, how to learn, how to interact with people, and so forth. We trust that if a human passed lots of classes and earned a degree, they must be able to function autonomously in society. Chad breaks these priors, so we need to rethink how to guarantee that doing well in classes implies functioning students coming out.

Cheating is now a blurry line

All the risks above are without cheating! When you include full-on cheating (which of course will happen), the situation gets even worse! Cheating is insidious now because it's virtually undetectable in many cases, so you can't properly enforce it. This puts immense pressure on honest students; imagine every math pset you submit is competing against IMO-gold-level LLMs for the curve! Even if you went through the class doing assignments honestly, you might get a worse score than everyone else (there are already some Reddit posts from teachers joking about how maybe the student with the worst scores is the only one that actually did the work).

Long live higher education

So how do we deal with this? The honest answer is nobody has got a clue, but there are some good ideas floating out there. I've described the ones I think are promising here.

Preventing the pocket TA

I think the trickiest part is finding a way to discourage or prevent over-use of a pocket TA, and forcing students to struggle and be comfortable with it, then discuss with their peers for help. It's difficult to prevent them from using LLMs for excessive hints like laying out the algorithm steps to implement, for checking every idea they have instantly towards a proof, or for coaxing them towards the right reagents for an end product. I honestly don't have good ideas towards this, except making exams harder to require true understanding of material rather than regurgitation. A reasonable approach could be to do our best to encourage students to work together, and then hopefully students will enjoy talking and learning from each other more than chat. Perhaps homeworks should be ungraded altogether, to encourage learning material rather than getting the right answers?

The teacher side of the arms race

One big plus side is that AI is incredibly useful for making course materials, and I think it could be a real renaissance for improving courses with high-effort materials. LLMs are (currently) pretty bad at generating materials from scratch, but they're incredibly useful for generating previously labor-intensive things like visualizations, LaTeX formatting, or interactive demos. I think the motivated instructor has a real opportunity to make world-class materials (not AI slop) with clever usage of LLMs. In addition, LLMs have a lot of potential to streamline burdensome course logistics like room scheduling, OH sign-ups, course webpage management, homework submissions, etc., or even offload large parts of grading entirely. This could let instructors devote time to figuring out how to structure their class and make broad-sweeping changes without worrying about the overhead of implementing them.

The bandaids

Something clearly has to be done quickly to prevent evaluation from falling apart. There are a few strategies for this, which many instructors are already implementing. Unfortunately, many of these strategies require taking on even more work for evaluation (like interviews), which many schools like Berkeley are poorly equipped to do with their current student:teacher ratios. In general, the bandaids all center around making it harder for students to shortcut thinking and still pass. In-person exams, oral interviews, randomly selected homework interviews, handwritten essays, and things of the like are probably a necessary intermediate step before we figure out what to do longer term. The bandaids are also very different depending on the subject, for example:

Coding/algorithms: Some classes are introducing trojan horses into programming assignments which tattle on students when they use LLMs in the assignment, or forcing them to use VSCode extensions which log keystrokes, and so on.

Writing: There are pretty good AI writing detectors (GPTZero, Pangram) which could be a first line of defense to require a score under 20% or something, or get an F. Beyond this, it's not really possible to enforce that students don't use AI for bouncing off ideas, finding sources, and whatnot. This part isn't so bad though, and maybe requiring them to discuss more in person could encourage them to engage personally with the material more.

Math: I quite liked a concept in CMU's CS theory class called "writing sessions," where everyone was required to hand-write a random subset of proofs from the pset within 45 mins. This at least forces the students to be able to explain the steps to the proofs even if they got them from an LLM.

Radical ideas

I've been wondering about what a bold restructuring of classes could result in. Here are some shower thoughts on directions for radically AI-forward classrooms.

Ungraded homeworks

One interesting concept is removing grades from homework. Assignments can be marked for correctness and given feedback the same way as now, but the only part that actually reflects a student's grade is in-person performance. Then the difficult part is figuring out how to get a low-noise signal on students' understanding.

In-person interviews

An idea that gets floated a lot is relying at least somewhat on interviews with course staff for assessing how well students actually know the homeworks. It has some issues with grade standardization, but in general I agree it's probably worth trying out and iterating on. Maybe some merging of homework writing sessions with interviews could achieve this? You put students in homework groups of 4, then each week you randomly pick one that has to explain the homework problems, and grade them all based on that person. This amortizes the interview cost for large classes and also gives students a good incentive to do homeworks together and make sure everyone understands it.

AI grading

If you allow LLMs to grade students, this opens up some interesting options. Maybe instead of submitting assignments, every student has to record a 10-minute video explaining the solutions to the problem set. Ordinarily this would be intractable to grade but maybe you could transcribe + grade with an LLM, and then manually grade only a random subset + regrades for quality control. At its extreme, an LLM could literally be used as the interviewer, where each student has to record a live-chat being asked questions about homework, and then transcripts are graded. This probably would take some iterating to make actually valuable, but doesn't seem too out of reach.

AI unchained

I've been trying to imagine an AI classroom taken to the extreme. You could imagine a scenario where students have nearly unlimited usage of AI. There are some interesting ways you could take this which all feel a little unsettling because they're so different. They also probably only work for upper-level classes when students already know fundamentals of their field.

  1. Grade students on real, worthwhile output. For example, maybe to get an A in compilers they have to get a PR merged into GCC or Clang.
  2. Make classes extremely hard, so that it's nontrivial to pass even for Claude. In practice this is really difficult to come up with, but at least in my field (robotics) one could do this with really meaningful full-stack projects which require hardware + software understanding.

Conclusion

To sum up, I think the main point of higher education is getting students to interact with each other to learn how to be a well-functioning part of society, how to struggle through problems, and how to meta-learn the art of learning a new topic. These "soft" skills are at extreme risk with AI, particularly through the easy access all students have to an on-demand pocket TA. In the short term, there's some patches instructors can implement to help, but I think there needs to be more discourse about re-vamping the canonical structure of classes to adapt with creative approaches to teaching. I certainly don't have the answers to how, but I described a few things I think seem interesting to try out.
